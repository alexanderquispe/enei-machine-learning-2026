\problem[5] How do the transition and emission matrices from 2C and 2D compare? Which do you think provides a more accurate representation of Ron's moods and how they affect his music choices? Justify your answer. Suggest one way that we may be able to improve the method (supervised or unsupervised) that you believe produces the less accurate representation.
\begin{subsolution}

The supervised model (2C) is likely more accurate. In 2C, we have access to both the hidden states (moods) and the observations (genres), so the transition and emission matrices are computed directly via maximum likelihood estimation, and each row corresponds to a known mood.

\textbf{Transition matrix comparison:} The supervised $A$ has all entries on the order of $10^{-1}$ to $10^{-2}$, meaning every mood-to-mood transition has nonzero probability (e.g., happy$\to$mellow $\approx 0.471$, sad$\to$angry $\approx 0.429$). The unsupervised $A$ has several near-zero entries such as $A_{1,2} \approx 3.7 \times 10^{-8}$ and $A_{3,3} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{-14}$, effectively making certain transitions impossible. This sharper structure means the unsupervised model has learned more deterministic state transitions, but these states don't necessarily correspond to the true moods.

\textbf{Observation matrix comparison:} The supervised $O$ spreads probability across most genres for each mood (smallest entry $\sim 2.4 \times 10^{-3}$), reflecting that every mood can produce any genre with some probability. The unsupervised $O$ has extremely sparse entries: e.g., $O_{1,2} \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-17}$ and $O_{3,5} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-25}$, meaning each hidden state is strongly specialized to emit only a subset of genres. State 0, for instance, concentrates $\sim 80\%$ of its emission probability on just genres 0 and 6.

\textbf{Which is better?} The supervised model more accurately represents Ron's moods because its states have a direct, interpretable mapping to the true moods. The unsupervised model's 4 hidden states are arbitrary and do not correspond one-to-one to the actual moods---they may represent permuted, merged, or split versions of the true states. Furthermore, Baum-Welch only guarantees convergence to a local maximum of the likelihood, not the global one, so the result is initialization-dependent.

\textbf{Improving the unsupervised method:} Run Baum-Welch with multiple different random initializations (seeds) and select the model that achieves the highest training data log-likelihood. This reduces the risk of converging to a poor local optimum.

\end{subsolution}

The \textbf{A matrix} (10$\times$10) is moderately sparse: each row has only a few entries with significant probability, while most are near zero. \indent\problem[5] % indent for consistency
How do the sample emission sentences from the HMM change as the number of hidden states is increased? What happens in the special case where there is only one hidden state? In general, when the number of hidden states is unknown while training an HMM for a fixed observation set, can we increase the training data likelihood by allowing more hidden states?

\begin{subsolution}

As the number of hidden states increases, the sample emissions become more structured and coherent:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1 hidden state:} The model reduces to a simple unigram (bag-of-words) model since there is only one state with a single emission distribution. The generated sentence is essentially random words sampled according to their overall frequency in the Constitution, with no sequential structure: \texttt{"Having of elections or or together be paid like vice a of excepting and duty..."}

    \item \textbf{2 hidden states:} The model can capture two broad word categories and alternate between them. The output begins to show slight groupings of related words but remains largely incoherent: \texttt{"Numbers composed by shall may shall for by by amendment congress be congress..."}

    \item \textbf{4 hidden states:} More structure emerges. The model can represent several distinct word clusters, and transitions between them produce sequences with recognizable fragments of constitutional language: \texttt{"May the senate 2 direct appointment of every time states by the states among the states..."}

    \item \textbf{10 hidden states (baseline):} The sentences show even more structure, with common constitutional phrases appearing: \texttt{"Of the use or for three united section made enter of this electors which the regulations..."}

    \item \textbf{16 hidden states:} The model has enough states to capture finer-grained word groupings. The output continues to show constitutional vocabulary with moderate coherence: \texttt{"Word states against and of either militia they which be no person shall elected composed of inability..."}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{In general}, yes, increasing the number of hidden states can increase
 the training data likelihood. More hidden states give the model greater
  capacity to fit the observed sequences, since the model can assign 
  different emission distributions to different states and use
   transition probabilities to capture more complex sequential
    patterns. However, this comes at the risk of overfitting: 
    with too many states, the model may memorize the training 
    data rather than learning generalizable structure.

\end{subsolution}
